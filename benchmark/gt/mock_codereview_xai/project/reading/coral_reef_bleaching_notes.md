# Reading notes: coral reef bleaching

Source pile: review articles and agency bulletins on marine heatwaves, plus two regional case reports. Notes written for my own reference, not for citation.

## 1. Basic mechanism

- Reef-building corals live in a mutualistic partnership with dinoflagellate algae of the family Symbiodiniaceae, often called zooxanthellae.
- The algae live inside coral tissue and supply most of the coral's energy through photosynthesis. In return the coral gives them shelter and nutrients.
- When water gets too warm, the photosynthetic machinery in the algae becomes damaged and produces reactive oxygen. The coral expels the algae, or the algae leave.
- Without the algae the coral tissue turns white, because the white calcium carbonate skeleton shows through the transparent tissue. This is what "bleaching" refers to.
- A bleached coral is not dead yet. It can recover if temperatures drop soon enough and the algae recolonize. If the stress lasts too long, the coral starves and dies.

## 2. Sea-surface temperature as the main driver

- Most bleaching in recent decades is linked to sea-surface temperature (SST) running a degree or two above the local summer maximum.
- Short spikes matter less than sustained warmth. A week of heat can be survived; several months of it usually cannot.
- Local factors such as shallow water, low water flow, and high irradiance can make a reef more vulnerable at the same SST.
- Some reefs in cooler upwelling zones are less exposed, but they are not immune. The reference point is always the local climatology, not a global number.

## 3. Degree heating weeks

- The main metric I kept coming back to is degree heating weeks (DHW). It accumulates thermal stress over a rolling 12-week window.
- Only anomalies at or above 1 degree C above the maximum monthly mean count toward the total. Cooler weeks do not cancel out earlier stress.
- Rough thresholds from the coral reef monitoring literature:
  - Around 4 DHW: significant bleaching becomes likely.
  - Around 8 DHW: widespread bleaching and substantial mortality are likely.
- The metric is useful because it compresses both intensity and duration into one number. It is also a simplification. Two reefs with the same DHW can respond differently depending on species mix and recovery time.
- Satellite-based products provide the SST inputs. Coverage is good over open reef systems but weaker in turbid nearshore water, where in situ loggers are still needed.

## 4. The 2016 event

- The 2016 event was the most widely reported bleaching episode up to that point. It was driven by a strong El Nino combined with long-term warming.
- Northern and central sections of the Great Barrier Reef showed the sharpest contrast in bleaching severity between sites, even within the same reef system.
- Surveys after the event found that many of the most heavily affected colonies were fast-growing branching species. Massive corals often held up better.
- Recovery in some sections was patchy. Reefs that were hit a second time before full recovery showed slower regrowth.
- Practical takeaway from the case reports: aerial surveys can map the spatial extent of pale reef quickly, but they cannot reliably tell bleached from dead coral without ground truthing.

## 5. The 2020 event

- The 2020 episode was again linked to heat stress, but it hit a more geographically dispersed set of reefs than 2016.
- Several reports stressed that multiple basins experienced significant heat stress in the same year, which made it harder for managers to allocate response effort.
- Symbiont loss was documented in fine detail for some species using microscopy and chlorophyll measurements. Recovery of symbiont density often took months after the water cooled.
- A recurring theme: bleaching itself is a visible signal, but the underlying loss of energy reserves can lead to reduced reproduction in the following years even when colonies survive.

## 6. Symbiont loss and recovery

- Recovery depends on whether the coral can reacquire compatible symbionts from the surrounding water or from its own remaining population.
- Some corals host several symbiont types that differ in heat tolerance. Shifts toward more tolerant types have been proposed as one adaptation pathway, though the evidence is mixed about how fast this can happen.
- Energy budgets matter. A coral that bleaches while already stressed by sedimentation or poor water quality has less capacity to recover.

## 8. Quick reference

| Term | Short meaning |
|---|---|
| Zooxanthellae | Symbiotic algae living in coral tissue |
| Bleaching | Loss of algae or their pigments, leaving pale tissue |
| SST anomaly | Temperature above the local seasonal maximum |
| DHW | Accumulated heat stress over 12 weeks, counting only anomalies of 1 degree C or more |
| Recovery | Return of symbionts after the stress ends |
